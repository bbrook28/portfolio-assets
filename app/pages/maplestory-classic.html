Case study 1 · Level design

Rules measured from the original maps

The problem

New maps in a classic game get compared with the old ones, and "this feels off" is not something a team can review. I needed a standard that anyone could check.

What I did

I wrote a tool, MapView, that reads map data and measures it. I ran it over about 90 of Nexon's original Victoria Island maps and recorded what they actually do: how wide the platform is under a large monster, how close monsters spawn to NPCs and portals, and how much of each floor is taken up by monsters alive at once. Those numbers became the region's rulebook.

What the data changed

My first rules were too cautious. I had big monsters restricted to 700 px platforms; the originals use platforms as small as 355 px, so the rule became 350. I had a 200 px buffer around NPCs; the originals spawn monsters 92 px away and right on top of portals, so the rule became 90. Where my rule and the original maps disagreed, the original maps won.

The result

I audited every map in the region against the rulebook three weeks before launch. Seven maps were too empty, so I raised their spawn rates and added 59 spawn points. Quest camps were cleared of monsters. One floor of Decayed Tunnel III was 75% crowded; moving three spawns to the next map brought it to 47% without changing the kill count a quest depends on. Every change went to QA with a rendered before and after.

Case study 2 · Level design

Rebuilding the region after the first playtest

The problem

The first versions of the maps went into a team playtest at the end of July. Two things came up again and again: how many monsters each floor had, and whether every class could hunt there. Some players camped a single platform for hours. In an MMO a map is somewhere people live, and my first layouts were built for passing through.

What I did

I rebuilt Precipice of Darkness the same night from what I had watched. Then I wrote a map standard for the whole region: maps had to work for solo players, parties and crowds; have one clear main route plus alternates and shortcuts; give players safe places to rest and regroup; and never trap players, pets or dropped items. I broke it into a checklist per map and reworked seven maps against it in three weeks, some from the ground up.

Example: Hanging Gardens of Rot

Every platform got a way in and a way out, and the map was split by job, marked below. Before launch I added 11 spawn points up top so the climb was worth making.

Tools

Tools I wrote for the team

The game's data is edited by hand, and mistakes only show up in the client. I wrote four Python tools to catch them earlier, published them to the team's tool repository and documented them.

MapView

Draws any map from the game data, clean or with footholds, portals and spawns, and renders a before and after of each change. Flags floors that run past the camera, walls too close to portals, drop-downs through solid rock and overcrowded floors. It caught small problems early, which cut two or three passes from QA. Every map image on this page came from it.

NPC Hi-Res

Exports NPC, monster and item sprites at four times their size, as PNGs, layered PSDs and pose sheets, plus a reference pack for outside artists. The marketing team used it instead of spending days digging through files for art.

Quest Tool

Compares each quest's written spec on the wiki with the quest data and lists every place they disagree. Its tester runs test plans through quests and party quests in a local client. Writing a plan and running it myself saved tens of hours.

Dev Tool

One window that sets up and starts the local servers and test client for a playtest, and saves hours of syncing across every repository.

I also wrote the team's dialogue style guide, a quest data guide for new designers, and a decision log of every design ruling.

Quest design

Seven questlines, one region

A five-quest opening act brings players down from Ellinia. Five questlines then open on separate level gates between 39 and 55, so a player always has one line at their level and a reason to go one map deeper. Each line's objectives are placed to walk the player through maps they have not seen yet.

Classic World is a social grinding game, and other players can steal your kills. I was careful about where each objective sent people and how crowded those maps would get. I also kept the dialogue short, so players read it instead of clicking past it.

Encounter design

The field boss

Field boss · Lv 60

Rotten Mushmom

  • An undead Mushmom, 20 levels above the Victoria Island original.
  • She only spawns in Someone Else's Grave, a hidden map off Decayed Tunnel I. Players find her by exploring, or through Glimmer's questline.
  • She regenerates and is weak to holy and fire, the same classes the rest of the Deep Passage favors. That makes her a party fight.
  • I wanted her to be a climactic moment players stumble into. Field boss maps fill up fast in this game, so hiding her and tying her to a questline kept the crowd off her map for a while.
