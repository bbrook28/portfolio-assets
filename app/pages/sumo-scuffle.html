Origin

From a class project to a commercial release

Sumo Scuffle started as a four-person SCAD prototype that I designed and programmed. I then rebuilt it alone as a full commercial release: design, programming, art and audio direction, playtesting, App Store submission, and marketing.

The goal was a game anyone can pick up at a party. That meant short bouts, simple controls, and chaos that stays readable when four people are crowded around one phone.

Playtesting · Designing for non-gamers

What playtesting changed

I playtested with people who rarely play games, and each round reshaped how the game is controlled and taught.

  1. Tilt by default

    Testers fought the joystick, so I added tilt controls: tip the phone to roll, tap anywhere to charge. They're now the default.

  2. Flick to charge

    One tester held the phone in one hand and steered with a finger they never lifted, so they couldn't charge. I added flick-to-charge and an in-game tip that teaches it.

  3. Play first, choose later

    New players were asked to pick controls before they'd moved at all. Now the Dojo starts them rolling straight away, asks "Like these controls?" afterwards, and lets them switch schemes live.

  4. Tips that step aside

    Lesson tips covered what testers were trying to do, so they now shrink out of the way once you start moving.

  5. Reward the aim

    A tester mashed the charge button, so missed charges now recharge more slowly.

  6. Show the gesture

    First-time players get a short lesson with an animated demo of each control scheme. A new scheme has to be tried once before they can keep it.

  7. Let players tune it

    A sensitivity slider covers stick, tilt, follow, and gamepad, so each player can set how quickly their sumo turns.

Modes · Readable chaos

The four modes

Four players and falling food can turn into noise fast. Every system is there to keep the bout readable and the score close.

  • Power-upsSix of them, built on sumo food and festival themes: Wasabi Rush, Giant Mochi, Magnet Belly, Belly Flop, Konnyaku Slick, and Taiko Frenzy. Each one is easy to read: a short slow-down and a light pillar on pickup, then a colored ring under your sumo that blinks before it wears off.
  • RicochetsPinball-style drums bounce wrestlers around the ring
  • Catch-upAn aura on whoever is behind, a crown on the leader, and a double-points final rush with a Last Bite countdown
  • ReplayAn automatic slow-motion replay of each bout's best moment, which players can share as a video clip
  • SurprisesMid-bout events that shake up a match
  • SeasonsYokai Night and a Winter Festival, each with its own arena, food, and music, and cosmetic rewards players keep
  • ReadabilityColorblind cues on top of color: star-shaped edges on power-up rings and spikes on bad food

Systems design

Adaptive difficulty

A hidden skill rating tunes the CPU opponents after every bout, so nobody can pick Easy and coast. Each CPU also picks a play style for the bout (greedy, bully, drummer, or careful), and the rating now reaches an Expert tier for strong players.

  • iOSCustom extension for Game Center, sharing, reminders, and deep links
  • BuildsAutomated straight to TestFlight
  • StatsOptional, anonymous play stats, asked for once, that show where new players drop out of the tutorial
  • BalanceA simulation tool for tuning the modes
